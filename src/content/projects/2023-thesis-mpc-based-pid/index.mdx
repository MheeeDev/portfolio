## Problem

Most PID loops in industry are tuned once, by trial and error, at one operating point — and then
left to run across the whole range. Model predictive control (MPC) copes with changing process
behaviour much better, but it needs a model, and it rarely reaches the PLCs and SCADA systems that
plants already own.

The thesis asked how far that can go on standard industrial hardware. **Phase I** laid the
foundation on a real pressure loop: measure how the process behaves across its range, turn that
into a model, and tune PID from the model — the baseline a Phase II MPC-based PID would be measured against.

## My role & scope

B.Eng. thesis in Chemical Process Engineering and Technology at KMUTNB, as a team of three. Our work on the pressure rig:

- **Step tests** — open-loop tests across the full valve range and the process-parameter calculations
- **Model** — a first-order-plus-dead-time (FOPDT) model of the pressure process at each operating point
- **Tuning** — PID settings by Ziegler–Nichols for every point, with Internal Model Control (IMC) set up as the comparison
- **On the PLC** — running the tuned loop on PID_Compact and recording the trends

## Approach

### The rig

A small but complete industrial loop — the same parts a plant would use:

- **Actuator:** control valve · **Measurement:** pressure transmitter
- **Controller:** Siemens S7-1200 with **PID_Compact** in TIA Portal V17
- **Operator panel:** WinCC Unified Comfort Panel
- **Data:** SIMATIC IoT2050 logging every test to CSV
- **Rehearsal:** Factory I/O to try the sequence before touching the rig

### Where MPC fits

The control architecture has four layers — field, automation, management and supervisory. Phase I
covers the bottom two: the valve and transmitter, and the PLC with its panel. In Phase II, MPC sits at
the supervisory level and adjusts the PID in the PLC instead of replacing it — a hierarchical structure
taken from published work on MPC-based PID for industrial plants (Aboelhassan et al., 2020).

### Model the process

A mass balance on the pressure vessel, taken to the Laplace domain and reduced to FOPDT:

```
G(s) = K · e^(−θs) / (τs + 1)
```

### Step tests across the whole range

With the controller in manual, the valve output was stepped from 0 % to each of 10, 20 … 100 %.
From each reaction curve: process gain `K` = ΔPV % / ΔMV %, dead time `θ` from the tangent at the
steepest point, and time constant `τ` at 63.2 % of the change.

| Step (MV %) | K | θ (s) | τ (s) | ZN PID: PB % | Ti (s) | Td (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 → 20 | 0.60 | 4.38 | 23.3 | 9.4 | 8.8 | 2.2 |
| 0 → 40 | 0.87 | 3.84 | 20.9 | 13.3 | 7.7 | 1.9 |
| 0 → 60 | 0.92 | 3.65 | 21.1 | 13.2 | 7.3 | 1.8 |
| 0 → 80 | 0.93 | 3.50 | 20.5 | 13.1 | 7.0 | 1.8 |
| 0 → 100 | 0.97 | 3.80 | 21.7 | 14.0 | 7.6 | 1.9 |

*Selected rows from ten tests. Ziegler–Nichols open-loop rules: PB % = 83·K·θ/τ, Ti = 2θ, Td = 0.5θ.*

Two things stand out. Where the process is steady, **τ/θ ≈ 5–6**: dead time is short next to the
time constant, so the loop is easy to tune. But **K is not constant** — above 30 % it sits at
0.85–0.97, below 20 % it falls to 0.30–0.60. The process is non-linear across the valve range.

So what happens when a PID tuned at one point runs at another? Below is the thesis loop as a

Things to try:

- **IMC tuned at 60 %, run at 10 %.** The loop gain drops to a third of its design value and settling
  stretches from about 22 s to over a minute — the low-gain region the thesis found.
- **Ziegler–Nichols tuned at 20 %.** Run it anywhere above 30 % and the loop gain is about 1.5× the design:
  the quarter-decay response turns into an oscillation that does not settle.
- **Switch on gain scheduling.** Scaling Kc by K<sub>tuned</sub> / K<sub>here</sub> brings the middle and top of
  the range back in line. At the bottom (10–20 %) the valve runs out of travel, so new gains alone are not enough there.

### Tune and run it on the PLC

The settings were loaded into PID_Compact in TIA Portal V17 and the loop was run against a 50 %
setpoint, with the reaction curve (ΔMV = 50 %) and the closed-loop response recorded on the PLC trend.

## Result

- Above 30 % valve output the process is consistent — `K` 0.85–0.97, `θ` 3.5–4.6 s,
  `τ` 20.5–22.8 s — and one PID tuning (PB ≈ 13–16 %, Ti ≈ 7–9 s, Td ≈ 1.8–2.3 s) fits it
- Below 20 % the gain falls to 0.30–0.60, so a PID tuned for the upper range is too gentle there:
  the measured evidence that the PID has to adapt to the operating point
- The habit this project started — measure the process, then tune from a model — runs through
  my later work, such as the [digital twin rig](../2025-digital-twin-rig/)

## Looking back

Three years on, I re-checked every slide. The measurements and the Ziegler–Nichols numbers hold up;
a few choices I would make differently today:

- **Start from IMC / SIMC, not Ziegler–Nichols.** ZN aims for quarter-decay oscillation — fine as a
  textbook reference, too aggressive as the first setting on a real valve.
- **Step around the operating point, up and down.** Stepping from 0 % every time includes the closed-valve
  region with the most deadband and stiction; small 5–10 % steps both ways give sharper local models.
- **Gain scheduling before MPC.** A gain that moves with valve opening is usually answered first with
  scheduling or valve-characteristic compensation. MPC earns its place with constraints, interacting loops or long dead time.
- **Check the gain units in PID_Compact.** A %/% gain must be converted when PV is scaled in engineering units.

The full review in Thai — step-test method, ZN against IMC λ, a tuning simulator and all eight
corrections — is in the [blog article](/blog/pid-tuning-step-test-to-imc-lambda/).
